01 / THE ECONOMICS

What is Personalised Pricing?

Have you ever noticed how the price of a flight or a product changes every time you check? That everyday experience captures the essence of pricing adjustments in digital markets.

Personalised pricing uses consumer information and estimated willingness to pay to differentiate prices between customers.

It is important to distinguish personalised pricing from dynamic pricing, where prices change over time in response to demand, capacity or other market conditions without necessarily varying by consumer.

Three Forms of Price Discrimination

FIRST DEGREE

Individual Valuation

Each consumer is charged their maximum willingness to pay. Under perfect first-degree discrimination, the seller captures the consumer surplus.

SECOND DEGREE

Self-Selection

Consumers select among quantities, product versions or pricing menus according to their preferences.

THIRD DEGREE

Group-Based Pricing

Different identifiable consumer groups face different prices, such as age-based or geographic discounts.

Note: Dynamic pricing is distinct from third-degree price discrimination. The categories above follow the standard economic classification.

02 / DIGITAL TRANSFORMATION

When Did the Debate Intensify, and Why?

The contemporary debate over personalised pricing intensified as digital markets underwent a structural transition driven by three developments: real-time algorithmic pricing, increasingly granular consumer data, and greater visibility of asymmetric pricing outcomes.

Algorithmic Pricing

Automated systems enable prices to respond rapidly to market signals and predicted demand.

Granular Consumer Data

Digital platforms can collect increasingly detailed behavioural information that may be used to estimate willingness to pay.

Asymmetric Price Outcomes

Consumers may encounter different offers without fully understanding why prices differ.

Between 2023 and 2025, public discussion around algorithmic pricing practices at major digital platforms and firms brought these mechanisms into greater focus.

This marked a shift from price discrimination as a theoretical construct to a practical governance challenge within digital markets.

The public debate is not only about economic efficiency. It also concerns whether consumers perceive pricing decisions as legitimate and fair.

The resulting questions extend across welfare economics, law, behavioural science and market regulation: How much differentiation can an algorithm introduce before it undermines consumer trust?

03 / ALGORITHMIC FAIRNESS

When Does a Price Become Unfair?

At the heart of the debate lies a simple but powerful question: When does a price become unfair?

Esposito and Grochowski (2024) propose the concept of laesio algorithmica, exploring how legal fairness principles might respond to algorithmically determined prices.

In digital markets, price differentiation can occur silently, dynamically and asymmetrically, making it difficult for consumers to understand or challenge an individual offer.

Key Dimensions of Laesio Algorithmica

  • Algorithmic fairness: Prices may raise concerns when they appear excessively high or exploitative relative to relevant benchmarks.
  • Technological context: Automated decisions can be opaque and difficult for consumers to negotiate.
  • Legal accountability: Updated standards may be needed to assess unfair algorithmic outcomes.
  • Consumer protection: Individuals need meaningful information and appropriate ways to challenge harmful pricing practices.

Fairness is therefore not merely an ethical consideration. It can become an economic constraint on firms' pricing strategies.

Markets may optimise prices, but consumers still expect fairness, transparency and legitimacy.

04 / BEHAVIOURAL ECONOMICS

How Personalised Pricing Can Undermine Consumer Trust

According to Vomberg's (2024) study of algorithmic dynamic pricing, opaque data-driven pricing mechanisms can have substantial implications for consumer trust and market behaviour.

Experimental evidence discussed in the study suggests that non-transparent algorithmic price adjustments may erode trust, particularly when prices appear personalised or unpredictable.

Why Trust Matters Economically

Consumers who suspect unfair pricing may respond in different ways.

  • More searching: Consumers compare additional sellers and prices to verify whether an offer is fair.
  • Higher information costs: Additional comparisons require time and cognitive effort.
  • Market disengagement: Some consumers may postpone purchases or avoid platforms they no longer trust.

These responses illustrate how perceived fairness can influence market participation and the effectiveness of pricing strategies.

A pricing algorithm may improve short-run revenue while creating longer-run costs if consumers lose trust in the platform.

From a governance perspective, transparency, explainability and consumer comprehension are therefore important design considerations.

05 / POLICY & GOVERNANCE

A Playbook for Fair and Trustworthy Algorithmic Pricing

The literature points toward several complementary approaches for reducing fairness risks while retaining the potential benefits of algorithmic pricing systems.

01 / TRANSPARENCY

Explain Pricing Rules

Communicate meaningful information about how prices are determined. Clearer disclosure can help reduce uncertainty and distrust.

02 / NON-DISCRIMINATION

Audit Pricing Outcomes

Examine protected attributes and potentially discriminatory proxies. Test for direct and indirect adverse outcomes.

03 / DATA ROBUSTNESS

Monitor Model Drift

Assess changing data distributions, covariate shift and model performance over time.

04 / FAIR OPTIMISATION

Design Fairness Constraints

Incorporate explicit fairness objectives or constraints into algorithmic optimisation.

05 / OVERSIGHT

Build Accountability

Establish documentation, internal review procedures, monitoring and clear responsibility for pricing decisions.

Final Thoughts

The future of personalised pricing will not be determined by algorithmic capacity alone, but by the institutional choices that shape how algorithms operate.

Transparency, non-discrimination, data robustness and fairness-aware optimisation are central to designing pricing systems that consumers can trust.

The challenge for digital markets is to build pricing architectures that can optimise efficiently while sustaining the trust on which market participation depends.
