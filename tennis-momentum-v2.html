03 / BEHAVIOURAL ECONOMICS

The Psychology of Momentum in Tennis.

A Behavioural-Economic Synthesis

Exploring how belief updating, strategic incentives, loss aversion, pressure and machine-learning evidence help explain momentum in competitive tennis.

ASLI UMAY YARDIMCI DECEMBER 23, 2025 BEHAVIOURAL ECONOMICS / SPORTS ANALYTICS
BELIEFS × INCENTIVES × BEHAVIOUR
Introduction Literature Framework Evidence Conclusion References
01 / INTRODUCTION

Is momentum more than a feeling?

Momentum is one of the most cited concepts in competitive tennis. Players, commentators and coaches routinely describe matches as shifting, swinging or turning, implying that recent success alters future performance.

While early psychological theories framed momentum as a subjective feeling of being "on a roll," recent advances in behavioural economics, sports analytics and machine learning have transformed momentum into a measurable and theoretically grounded construct.

Central research question: What mechanisms generate momentum, how can it be quantified, and how does it influence performance under risk and pressure?

This paper develops a theoretical synthesis integrating belief updating, loss aversion, overconfidence and pressure-induced performance shifts with empirical evidence from econometrics and machine learning.

Building on research that distinguishes strategic momentum from psychological momentum, the study proposes an integrated behavioural-economic framework explaining how these mechanisms interact.

02 / LITERATURE REVIEW

What does the research tell us?

2.1 Psychological Momentum and Belief Updating

Psychological momentum refers to changes in performance following recent success that are driven by confidence, expectations and perceived control. Early research described momentum as a self-reinforcing internal state.

Klaassen and Magnus (2001) applied a dynamic binary panel data model to professional tennis and rejected the hypothesis that points are independently and identically distributed. Their findings established an important statistical basis for investigating momentum.

Meier et al. (2020) used exogenous interruptions, including medical timeouts and weather delays, to distinguish psychological from strategic momentum. They found that the performance boost following a converted break point disappears after an interruption.

Because these interruptions do not change the score or strategic incentives, the result supports a psychological mechanism involving confidence and belief updating.

2.2 Strategic Momentum and Rational Effort

Strategic momentum can arise from optimal effort allocation under tennis scoring rules. Gauriot and Page (2019) show that players respond to point importance by adjusting effort.

High-leverage situations such as deuce and break points may therefore generate clusters of strong performance without requiring a psychological explanation.

Depken et al. (2022) further examine strategic and psychological components of momentum.

2.3 Loss Aversion, Overconfidence and Pressure

Behavioural biases can change how players respond to competitive situations. Loss aversion may encourage conservative decisions when facing potential losses.

Anbarci et al. (2018) document safer serving strategies in critical situations, consistent with loss-averse behaviour.

Overconfidence can lead to excessive risk-taking after a sequence of successes. Conversely, pressure-induced choking can reduce execution quality when the stakes rise.

Research by Krumer et al. (2018) and Meffert et al. (2018) examines differences in performance under pressure.

2.4 Machine-Learning Approaches

Momentum is not directly observable. It must be inferred from indicators such as scoring sequences, serve quality, rally characteristics and error patterns.

Machine-learning methods can detect nonlinear relationships and interactions that are difficult to represent using simpler statistical models.

Zhong et al. (2024) combine logistic regression with a sparse ARMAX framework. Other studies examine random forests and XGBoost for momentum measurement and prediction.

Understanding ARMAX

ARMAX stands for Autoregressive Moving Average with Exogenous Variables. The approach models a variable using its past values, previous shocks and external predictors.

Mt = AR(past momentum) + MA(past shocks) + X(external factors)

Conceptual illustration of ARMAX components, not the exact estimated equation from the cited study.

  • Autoregressive component: Momentum depends on its own previous values.
  • Moving-average component: Previous unexpected shocks influence the current state.
  • Exogenous component: Serve advantage, score differences, fatigue and opponent errors may contribute.

LASSO regularisation can help select influential predictors and reduce overfitting when many correlated variables are available.

03 / THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

Four components. One dynamic system.

The proposed framework views each player as a dynamic decision-maker whose point-level performance emerges from interacting psychological, strategic and behavioural processes.

COMPONENT A

Psychological Momentum

Recent outcomes update beliefs, confidence and perceived control.

COMPONENT B

Strategic Incentives

Players adjust effort and actions according to the importance of points.

COMPONENT C

Behavioural Biases

Loss aversion, overconfidence and pressure influence decision quality.

COMPONENT D

Observable Momentum

Interacting mechanisms generate a latent momentum state that can be inferred from performance data.

Component A: Belief-Based Momentum

A player's subjective probability of winning can change after psychologically important events, such as converting a break point.

πt+1 = f(πt, recent outcomes, point importance)

Positive belief revisions may increase confidence and perceived control. This mechanism does not require changes in physical ability or strategic incentives.

Because it relies on a continuous cognitive and emotional sequence, belief-based momentum can be disrupted by unexpected interruptions.

Component B: Strategic Incentives

Rational players choose actions by considering expected benefits and costs. When the value of winning a point rises, greater effort may become optimal.

Expected utility = expected benefit − action cost

Simplified conceptual representation of the decision problem.

As a result, strong performance can cluster around important points even without psychological momentum.

Component C: Behavioural Biases

Loss aversion can induce overly conservative decisions. Pressure may reduce execution quality, while overconfidence may encourage excessive risk-taking.

These biases explain why two players facing similar scores and incentives may respond differently.

Component D: The Latent Momentum State

Momentum is conceptualised as an unobserved state emerging from the interaction of beliefs, incentives and behavioural biases.

Mt = αMt−1 + β1St + β2Pt + β3Bt + εt

S = strategic factors, P = psychological shocks, B = behavioural-bias triggers. This is a reconstructed conceptual notation because the pasted original equation contains corrupted symbols.

This representation connects the theoretical framework to dynamic statistical approaches such as ARMAX.

04 / EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

How does the evidence fit together?

4.1 Evidence for Psychological Momentum

Meier et al. (2020) provide evidence that a performance boost following break-point conversion disappears after an exogenous interruption. This finding is consistent with the belief-based mechanism described in Component A.

4.2 Evidence for Strategic Momentum

Gauriot and Page (2019), together with Depken et al. (2022), examine how scoring incentives and effort allocation generate momentum-like patterns.

These findings indicate that not every successful sequence should be interpreted as a psychological hot hand.

4.3 Evidence for Behavioural Biases

Research on loss aversion, choking and clutch performance suggests that players do not always behave as fully rational score maximisers.

Pressure sensitivity can moderate the relationship between positive momentum and actual performance.

4.4 Evidence from Machine Learning

Machine-learning studies use point histories, opponent errors and serve-related variables to infer momentum patterns.

Such models provide predictive evidence for the usefulness of momentum-related information, although prediction alone does not identify a specific psychological or causal mechanism.

Integrated interpretation: Momentum-like performance patterns may reflect psychological belief updating, rational effort allocation and behavioural biases operating simultaneously.

05 / CONCLUSION

Momentum as an emergent phenomenon.

This paper develops a unified behavioural-economic framework for understanding momentum in tennis. Rather than treating momentum as either entirely psychological or entirely strategic, it considers the interaction of three systems:

  • A psychological system of confidence and belief updating.
  • A strategic system of incentive-driven effort allocation.
  • A behavioural system in which biases and pressure affect decisions.

The resulting momentum state can be studied through econometric methods and machine-learning approaches, each contributing different kinds of evidence.

The main contribution of this synthesis is to organise findings from psychology, economics and data science within a coherent conceptual structure.

Future Research

Future work could jointly estimate belief states, incentive responses and behavioural bias parameters using structural econometric models or theory-informed machine-learning methods.

Separating point-importance variables from recent-success indicators would help researchers distinguish strategic effects from psychological mechanisms.

Momentum is not simply a winning streak. It is a dynamic interaction between what players believe, what the scoring system rewards, and how they respond under pressure.
